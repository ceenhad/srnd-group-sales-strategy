### The 3 Rs block

> ## The 3 Rs of acoustics
>
> Most acoustic systems open with physics. We would rather open with the problem, because there are only three
> that matter and each has its own answer.
>
> **Reflection** — sound arriving at the seat by the wrong route, smearing detail and pulling the image apart.
> → **Reflection Control Panel**
>
> **Resonance** — the room's own low-frequency behaviour, which makes bass strong in one seat and thin in the next.
> → **Resonance Control Panel**
>
> **Reverberation** — how long sound takes to die away, which decides whether dialogue is clear or muddled.
> → **Reverberation Control Panel**
>
> One panel per problem. Nothing to choose between competing approaches, nothing left to interpretation.

### The depth block

> ## 50 mm, and that is a commercial decision
>
> Deep treatment works. It also consumes floor area, and in the rooms we work in that space is worth considerably
> more than the treatment fitted to it. Every C-ATS panel is engineered to perform within 50 mm — shallower than
> anything comparable — so the acoustics do not cost the room its dimensions.
>
> Panels are designed to sit behind a stretch-fabric finish. The client sees the surface; the acoustics work
> underneath it.

### The proof block

> ## The performance is measured, and the report is published
>
> Every panel was tested by **BSRIA** to **BS EN ISO 354** in a 210 m³ reverberation chamber. The absorption
> coefficients are published per panel, and so is the **full test report** — not a summary of it.
>
> We publish it because a specification you can defend is worth more than a claim you have to take on trust.
>

## Product on-ramps

*Each replaces the current SKU-first title. The code belongs in the specification, not the headline.*

### Reflection Control Panel

> **The problem:** at the seat, the sound arriving off the side walls, rear wall and ceiling competes with the
> sound arriving from the speakers. Detail smears and the image loses its edges.
>
> **What it does:** redirects that reflected energy away from the listening position — scattering it off-axis
> rather than absorbing it, so the room keeps its life instead of going dead.
>
> **Why it matters to you:** it is the difference between a room that measures acceptably and one that sounds
> convincing. 300 × 300 × 50 mm, 16 to a box.
>
> **One thing to get right:** bond it as well as screwing it. The adhesive damps the panel so it works as a clean
> scatterer — screwed alone it behaves differently. One page in the install guide, and worth reading first.

### Resonance Control Panel

> **The problem:** below roughly 200 Hz the room stops behaving like a room and starts behaving like an
> instrument. Bass is heavy in one seat, absent in the next, and no amount of processing fixes a pressure problem.
>
> **What it does:** absorbs at low frequency where the pressure is highest — in the corners — so the modal
> behaviour is damped rather than equalised around.
>
> **Why it matters to you:** it is the problem most rooms never solve, and the one clients notice without being
> able to name. 600 × 600 × 50 mm, 4 to a box.
>
> **Measured in the configuration it is used in:** the published figures come from corner placement, not from a
> flat wall — which is how it is actually installed.

### Reverberation Control Panel

> **The problem:** sound that takes too long to die away. Dialogue loses intelligibility, effects run into each
> other, and the room sounds larger and less controlled than it is.
>
> **What it does:** absorbs across the mid and high band to bring decay time to target, distributed rather than
> concentrated so the room stays even.
>
> **Why it matters to you:** it is the most visible improvement to a client — the moment a room stops sounding
> like a room. 300 × 300 × 50 mm, 16 to a box, peel-and-stick.
>
> **One thing to get right:** the adhesive needs a room above 18 °C for 24 hours before installation. Fit it cold
> and it will let go later.
