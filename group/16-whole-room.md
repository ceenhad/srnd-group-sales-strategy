# The whole room

*What the four settled brand strategies add up to. Written after C-ATS, Fabric Walls, Display
Technologies and Pro-Fi were each worked through individually (`brands/*/positioning.md`), because the
group-level truth only became visible once they were. Internal.*

The group's proposition has always been described as "six brands, one relationship." That is accurate
but it undersells what the brand work has since established. The brands are not six independent lines
that happen to share an invoice. They are the layers of a single room, and each was designed against
a different part of the same problem.

## The layers

A high-end room resolves into a small number of jobs, and the portfolio maps onto them almost exactly.

**Fabric Walls is the surface.** It is the aesthetic layer of the room — the finished plane that
everything else is set into or concealed behind. It is the only brand the client sees, and it holds the
room's design together.

**Display Technologies integrates the technology into that surface.** Screens, masking, mounts,
concealment, port holes, and the front-of-room structure exist so the equipment serves the experience
without dominating the space. DT is where the hardware meets the architecture.

**C-ATS makes the room sound right.** Reflection, resonance and reverberation are controlled behind
the fabric, with the treatment designed to the room and the result measurable. Isolation belongs here
too, because C-ATS is where acoustics lives.

**Pro-Fi is the sound itself** — a complete audio system engineered as one stack, placed within the
structure and concealed by the surface, tuned to the client and able to put the sound where the design
needs it rather than where a speaker happens to fit.

**Lighting is the fifth layer**, and Light Walls is the brand that holds it. Its strategy is
deliberately not settled here; it is being worked separately and should not be pre-empted.

Read in that order, the room is not a collection of products. It is one system with a surface, a
picture, an acoustic and a sound — and the group makes all of it.

## Where the layers become one product

Two things in the portfolio are not cross-sell opportunities but genuine joint capabilities. They
matter more than the sum of the brand strategies, and neither belongs to a single brand.

**The Screen Wall is the whole-room proposition made physical.** A modular front-of-room structure with
a decoupled framework, baffle-mounted speaker positions and acoustic control: DT engineers the
structure, Pro-Fi's speakers sit within it, C-ATS's acoustics work through it, and a Fabric Walls
surface finishes it. One purchased product contains four brands. Nothing else in the portfolio
demonstrates "one relationship, whole room" as directly, which is why its current state is the group's
most conspicuous gap — it has been demonstrable since ISE 2023 and still has no product page,
datasheet or story. Productising it is a group priority, not a DT errand.

**The LED shift is answered jointly, or not at all.** Large flat panels and LED walls are displacing
projection from the top of the market, and an LED wall is not acoustically transparent — so the
speakers that used to sit behind the screen have nowhere to go. DT's Screen Wall provides the structure
and the speaker positions; Pro-Fi's Lattice decouples where sound appears to come from from where the
speakers physically are. Together they solve LED audio integration. Separately, neither does. This is
the clearest case in the portfolio where two brands' futures are the same move, and it should be
treated as a group capability to build and prove — today it is a real capability, not yet a
demonstrated track record, and must be described that way.

## The rules that keep it coherent

The brand work surfaced a small number of alignment rules. They exist because adjacent brands
naturally drift into each other's territory, and a group that contradicts itself loses the advantage
the coherence was supposed to create.

- **Acoustic treatment design belongs to C-ATS.** Fabric Walls carries acoustic behaviour physically,
  DT provides structure and decoupling, Pro-Fi handles electronic calibration and voicing — but the
  design of what a room needs acoustically is C-ATS's. The others cross-sell it; none duplicates it.
  (The Fabric Walls site currently offers a competing acoustic design service; that needs removing.)
- **No brand claims to be the most complex or critical part of the project.** Each element is
  demanding in its own terms. A superlative contest between our own brands reads as noise.
- **The dealer is the hero in every brand, and the mechanics may differ.** C-ATS keeps design as
  chargeable expertise sold as an accountable deliverable; Fabric Walls is self-serve by design and should
  have configurators. Same discipline, opposite mechanics — don't propagate one brand's tooling rule to
  another. (The earlier "C-ATS publishes no calculators" rule is withdrawn: tooling lives under the Cinema
  Tools master brand, and the real constraint is what a tool's placement *implies* —
  `20-tools-and-calculators.md`.)
- **The Scandinavian register governs all of it** — restraint, plain language, substance over
  adjectives, no superlative without a measurement behind it.

## Why this is worth the effort

The commercial argument is straightforward. A dealer won on any one layer is a dealer who can be served
on the other four, and the room is where the value compounds: one account, one delivery, one
relationship, and a set of products that were designed to meet each other rather than to coexist. That
is the moat expressed at product level (`14-moat.md`) — depth across the whole room, spent on the
dealer.

It also fixes the specific failure the group has been living with. The brand stories were divergent and
largely untold; each brand was strong in isolation and silent about the others. Coherence is the
correction, and it is a prerequisite rather than an end in itself.

## The part that isn't true yet

The whole-room argument has a practical form that is far stronger than any story: **buying the whole room
from us ought to be easier.** We would already hold the drawings, know how the pieces integrate, and have
made them to meet each other — one order, one delivery, no coordination burden falling on the dealer. No
competitor selling a single layer can answer that.

Today it is a claim rather than an experience. Tools and data are fragmented, so a dealer buying across
brands still assembles the picture themselves, and the proposition fails on exactly the ground where it
should be unbeatable. Much of what SRND Engine and the platform's canonical dataset set out to solve is
this fragmentation — which makes the whole-room advantage substantially an **operational** problem, not a
messaging one, and puts it squarely in the *institutionalise* strand of `15-activate.md`: depth that stays
fragmented cannot be spent on the dealer, which is part of why the moat is still latent.

The sequence follows the group's standing rule — build it, then say it. Make the room genuinely easier to
buy, and only then market the ease (`20-tools-and-calculators.md`, `open-items.md`).

## What it opens

With the story coherent across four brands, the next question is how a dealer actually enters it.
Nobody buys a whole room on first contact, and the proposition is too large to lead with — so the
practical work becomes an entry route: which layer opens the relationship, what a first order looks
like, and how a dealer moves from one brand to the room. That is the on-ramp, and it belongs with the
buyer journey (`03-buyer-journey.md`) rather than here.
