# The on-ramp

*How a dealer we've never met becomes an SRND partner, and how that partnership deepens — without
subordinating any brand to the group. Written after the whole-room synthesis (`16-whole-room.md`),
because the coherent story is what makes an entry route possible. Internal.*

## The tension this has to hold

The whole-room story is true and valuable, and it is a **group** story. At brand level it is worse than
unhelpful: no dealer buys Fabric Walls in order to become step one of something larger, and a brand
that presents itself as an entrance to a bigger proposition has been demoted. Each brand exists in its
own right, competes in its own category, and must be a complete proposition standing on its own merits.

Both layers are real, so neither can be ignored. The resolution is a division of labour:

**The dealer experiences brands. SRND operates the trail.**

The on-ramp is a group-side mechanism. It is our operating model, not the dealer's narrative. It should
be almost entirely invisible in brand-facing material.

## The hinge: registration and marketing permission

There is one moment when an unknown contact becomes a potential SRND partner, and it is specific: they
**register and give us permission to market to them.** Everything about the on-ramp arranges itself
around that boundary, because it is the point at which what we are allowed to do changes.

**Before the hinge — brand-led, public, attraction only.** We have no permission and no relationship, so
this work cannot be direct and cannot be a group pitch. It is done by the brands, in their own
categories, in public: the brand sites, the channels, the content, the search and AI visibility.
The group's role here is limited to the top-of-funnel introduction and routing already described in
`09-brand-portfolio.md` — brief, and pointing at whichever brand fits the need.

**The hinge itself — registration is the conversion that matters.** Not a first order: registration.
It is the moment we acquire the right to have a relationship, and it should be treated as the primary
metric of the on-ramp rather than a formality on the way to a basket. The mechanism already runs in
engine; what matters strategically is that registration is *worth something to the dealer* — access,
pricing, tools, support — so that permission is given willingly rather than extracted.

**After the hinge — SRND partner campaigns, permissioned and group-level.** Now the group can act
directly, because it has consent and identity. This is where the trail is worked: managed campaigns to
known partners, aware of what they've bought, which layer of the room they came in through, and which
adjacency is genuinely next. This is also where the whole-room story becomes appropriate — it is held
for engaged contacts, never used as the cold open (`03-buyer-journey.md`).

Two distinct campaign programmes follow from this, and they should not be conflated:

- **Brand acquisition campaigns** — external, public, brand-voiced, problem-led. Their job is to be
  found and to pique interest. No group pitch, no cross-sell.
- **Partner development campaigns** — internal to the relationship, permissioned, SRND-run,
  adjacency-aware. Their job is to deepen a known partner: the next layer, the training, the design
  service, the whole room.

## The door is a product, not a brand

The trail is not a queue, and entry is not really at brand level at all. A dealer's first purchase from
an unknown supplier is governed by one thing: **how much trust it requires.** What makes a door wide is
therefore a set of product characteristics, not a brand's breadth of application —

- low value and low risk,
- one obvious problem solved,
- easy to specify without help,
- and quick to prove in a real job.

By that measure the obvious first purchases are small and discrete: a hush box or a port hole, a couple
of Spatial speakers, a few acoustic panels, a Leyard display. **Fabric Walls is not the widest door**,
despite being on almost every room and self-serve by design — a fabric interior is expensive, large and
completely visible if it goes wrong, so buying one means trusting a stranger with the entire finished
surface of a client's room. That is a substantial act of faith, and it belongs later on the trail. Its
self-serve tooling makes it easy to *specify*; it does not make it easy to *risk*.

Two consequences follow.

**Every brand needs to know its entry product**, and that is a different question from its flagship. The
flagship demonstrates the depth; the entry product opens the relationship. For some brands the answer is
already obvious (DT's smaller mounts, enclosures and port holes; Pro-Fi's shipping Spatial cabinets), and
for others it needs deciding — including whether a small Fabric Walls element can serve as one at all.

**The first order's job is to prove we deliver, not to showcase what we can do.** At that moment the
dealer is testing whether we are reliable — lead time, packaging, accuracy, support when they ring. This
puts the weight on fulfilment rather than product sophistication, and it means a modest first order badly
handled costs far more than its value.

Beyond that first purchase, a dealer still arrives from whichever direction their current job takes them
— a fit-out contractor through fabric, a cinema specialist through acoustics or audio, a large-room
project through DT — so no brand is "step one" and none is subordinate.

**Leyard is an uncovered opportunity here.** It is the group's only genuine third-party line
(`09-brand-portfolio.md`) and, on this logic, potentially one of the strongest doors we have: an
established outside brand asks the least trust of all, because the dealer isn't betting on SRND's own
engineering in order to try us. No work has been done on it in this repo — a real gap, logged in
`open-items.md`.

What the group holds is therefore not a sequence but an **adjacency map** — given where a dealer came
in, what is the next layer of the same room, and what physical adjacency makes that next layer obvious.
The strongest adjacencies are the ones that are literally true in a build: the treatment goes behind the
fabric; the speakers sit within the front-of-room structure; the screen integrates into the surface.

**A single-brand dealer is not a failed dealer.** Some accounts will only ever be Fabric Walls, or
cinema-only specialists, and that is a good outcome, consistent with the dealer-as-asset thesis
(`01-dealer-as-asset.md`). If the trail treats them as incomplete it will push adjacency at people who
don't want it, which is precisely the oversaturation risk already flagged in `02-commercial-model.md`.

## What actually engages a dealer: the problem, not the product

This is the hard-won part, and it governs every piece of content on the on-ramp. **A product on its own
does nothing.** Experience says so plainly. What engages a dealer is:

- **What problem does this solve?**
- **How does it save me time?**
- **How does it make me more money?**
- **How does it make the experience better?**

The surprising and important observation is how little dealers understand **why** to specify something.
That gap is not a weakness to exploit; it is where engagement comes from. Explaining why to spec
something is the most valuable thing we can do in public, and it is what earns the interest that leads
to registration. The first job of any piece is simply to **pique interest** — not to inform
exhaustively, and certainly not to announce a product.

This is already the pattern the brands share, which is why it works as a group principle rather than an
imposition. C-ATS states three problems and gives three solutions. Fabric Walls sells the removal of
risk, effort and time. DT sells technology that integrates rather than dominates. Pro-Fi describes the
friction and then what it did about it. In each case the product is the answer to a stated problem —
never the opening subject.

The discipline that follows: lead with the problem, quantify the benefit in the dealer's terms (time,
margin, result), and let the specification follow for those who want it. Spec sheets serve the dealer
who has already decided; they do not create the decision.

## Where the group is allowed to surface

Because the group story is held back, it needs defined moments where it appears — earned, not asserted:

- **The shared account and single delivery.** One registration, one checkout, one delivery across
  independent brands. This is the most concrete group benefit and it is experienced, not claimed.
- **A physical adjacency inside a real project.** The most persuasive cross-sell is the one the build
  itself implies.
- **The Experience Centre.** Standing in a finished room is the whole-room story without a word of it.
- **The Screen Wall.** The one case where it is genuinely a single product rather than four brands sold
  together — which is why productising it matters to the on-ramp, not just to DT.

## The seam to design deliberately

`srnd.store` is where the two layers actually collide in the dealer's experience: one account and one
checkout across brands that are otherwise independent. It is the honest seam, and it cannot be resolved
by assertion — the store has to make the shared account feel like a benefit rather than make the brands
feel like departments. That is a design problem to work, not a position to state.

## What this demands next

- Define what registration **grants**, so permission is willingly given. This is the partner-program
  policy already open in `open-items.md`, and the on-ramp depends on it.
- **Choose each brand's entry product** — the low-risk, easy-to-specify item a stranger can buy to try
  us — and make sure fulfilment on those items is exemplary, because that first order is the test.
- **Work out what Leyard does on the on-ramp.** Uncovered so far, and possibly the lowest-trust door in
  the portfolio.
- Build the **adjacency map** properly: for each entry point, the next layer, the physical adjacency
  that justifies it, and the moment in a project when it is relevant.
- Separate the two campaign programmes in practice — brand acquisition (public, problem-led) and
  partner development (permissioned, adjacency-aware) — including who owns each.
- Set the on-ramp metric as **registrations with permission**, not first orders, and track progression
  along the adjacency map thereafter (`08-metrics.md`).
- Write the "why specify this" content as the core acquisition asset for each brand, in the brand's own
  voice. This is content-phase work (`15-activate.md`), and it is the highest-value content the group
  can produce.
