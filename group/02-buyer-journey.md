# The buyer journey and the on-ramp

*How a dealer we've never met becomes one who orders across the group. Defined once here; each brand runs
its own version over a largely shared dealer base, with its own content at each stage.*

## The arc

A dealer meets us at the top of the funnel — a search, a referral, a trade event, or another SRND brand they
already buy — drawn by a **single simple problem**, not the group's full story. They land on `srnd.group`,
which introduces the company briefly and points at the brand that fits. On the **brand site** they get the
functional case: does this solve my problem, can I spec and install it, will it hold up in front of my
client. Convinced, they register as a trade partner and buy through **`srnd.store`** — one account that
already covers every other brand. The first job goes in; we make it succeed. *Now* the whole-room story
earns its place, and the cross-sell opens. A satisfied dealer refers a peer, and it begins again — with the
group, not just the brand that opened the door.

## The six stages

1. **Unaware → get in front of them.** The relationship can start at any brand or at the group. Warmest of
   all is a dealer who already buys another SRND brand — our own base is a standing entry point. The top of
   funnel is deliberately wide; use that to spread contact load rather than funnelling everyone through one
   door. *Content: the hook — the core problem, stated boldly.*
2. **Name lands, means nothing → land the proposition.** One system, a real problem, made easy. They're on
   a brand site, meeting a brand backed by a group that covers the whole room. *Content: range and
   possibility.*
3. **"Could this work for me?" → prove it.** It works, it's easy to spec and install, it won't embarrass
   them in front of a client. The brand site's real job, backed by shared proof: knowledge-base how-to,
   virtual case studies, recorded demos, sample kits, the Experience Centre, measured data, a Zoom.
4. **Wants to buy → register, then buy.** Pricing is partner-only, so **registration is the gate** — and one
   account covers every brand. Register once, buy the whole room.
5. **First order, job on site → make the first job succeed.** Install support, verification, make the dealer
   look good. The project often already spans brands, so support what they actually bought. Outcome: a
   satisfied dealer *and* a public-safe reference.
6. **The next order — the hardest stage, and the group's payoff.** Process, not hope: reorder ease on a
   group-wide account; new reasons (a new element, or a range once it ships — never before); **cross-brand
   expansion**, where the whole-room story finally belongs; and persistent presence, because the dealer's
   next project lands on *their client's* clock, not ours. So it isn't a push — it's being recalled the
   moment it lands and being effortless to act on.

## Purchaser and specifier are different people

**Don't mistake what happens now for what should happen.** Today the order comes from the integrator and we
tend to help them spec — which is real, and is the relationship this journey describes. But on a project of
any size the specification is developed **pre-tender**, by **architects, AV consultants and interior
designers**. By the time an integrator is ordering, the naming has largely happened somewhere upstream, and
helping them spec is us working downstream of the decision.

So two roles, and the strategy has only ever addressed one:

- **The purchaser — the integrator.** Orders, installs, carries the client relationship, and is who the
  products are optimised for. Everything else in this document is aimed here, correctly.
- **The specifier — architect, AV consultant, interior designer.** Writes the documents in which products get
  named, before anyone tenders. We barely address them.

**Being named pre-tender is worth more than winning a spec conversation later**, because it inverts the
dynamic: the integrator has to buy what's specified, or argue their way out of it. It does not displace the
integrator — they still order, install and need supporting — it defends the position before the purchase
exists.

**And it is the same move as going beyond cinema.** Those specifiers work across commercial fit-out,
hospitality and workplace, not only cinema. Getting into pre-tender specification and reaching adjacent
markets are one piece of work, not two.

### What being specified actually requires

Construction specification has established machinery, and we do almost none of it:

- **RIBA-approved CPD seminars** — the standard route to architects in the UK.
- **NBS specification clauses** — being present in the system specifiers actually write in.
- **BIM / Revit objects** — architects design in a model; a product that can be dropped into it is a product
  that gets specified.
- **Specifier-grade technical downloads** — drawings, performance data, details, at the level a spec writer
  needs rather than a dealer.
- **Finish and sample libraries** for interior designers.
- **Approved-supplier and framework listings**, where they apply.

Two tensions to settle rather than drift into:

- **It qualifies a C-ATS line.** "Optimised for the integrator, not the acoustician" is right about who the
  *product* serves — but if acoustic consultants write pre-tender specs, treating them as a non-audience is a
  mistake. Two distinct questions had been merged: who the product is optimised for, and who influences
  specification.
- **It collides with gating design data.** Being specified means publishing drawings and model objects;
  guarding against a competitor copying our CAD (`06-competitors.md`) argues for locking them down. Both are
  legitimate. The answer needs deciding deliberately — plausibly: publish what a specifier needs, gate what a
  manufacturer would need.

## The on-ramp: registration is the hinge

There is one moment when an unknown contact becomes a potential partner, and it is specific: **they register
and give us permission to market to them.** Everything arranges around that boundary, because it is the point
at which what we're allowed to do changes.

- **Before the hinge — brand-led, public, attraction only.** No permission and no relationship, so this
  can't be direct and can't be a group pitch. The brands do it, in their own categories: sites, content,
  channels, search and AI visibility.
- **The hinge itself — registration is the conversion that matters.** Not a first order. It should be the
  primary metric of the on-ramp, and it has to be *worth something to the dealer* so permission is given
  willingly rather than extracted.
- **After the hinge — SRND partner development, permissioned.** Now the group can act directly, aware of
  what they bought and which adjacency is genuinely next. This is also where the whole-room story becomes
  appropriate.

**Two campaign programmes follow, and must not be conflated:** *brand acquisition* (external, public,
problem-led, no cross-sell) and *partner development* (permissioned, SRND-run, adjacency-aware).

**The dealer experiences brands; SRND operates the trail.** The on-ramp is our operating model, not the
dealer's narrative, and should be near-invisible in brand-facing material. No brand is a funnel step: a brand
that presents itself as an entrance to something bigger has been demoted.

### The door is a product, not a brand

A first purchase from an unknown supplier is governed by one thing: **how much trust it requires.** A wide
door is low value, low risk, solves one obvious problem, is easy to specify and quick to prove — a hush box,
a couple of Spatial cabinets, a few panels, a Leyard display.

**Fabric Walls is not the widest door**, despite being on almost every room and self-serve by design: a
fabric interior is expensive, large and completely visible if it goes wrong, so buying one means trusting a
stranger with the finished surface of a client's room. Self-serve tooling makes it easy to *specify*, not
easy to *risk*.

Two consequences. **Every brand needs an entry product**, distinct from its flagship — the flagship
demonstrates the depth, the entry product opens the relationship. And **the first order tests us, not the
product**: at that moment the dealer is judging lead time, packaging, accuracy and whether support answers.
The weight is on fulfilment, so a modest order handled badly costs far more than its value.

Beyond that first purchase, dealers arrive from whichever direction their current job takes them, so the
group holds an **adjacency map** rather than a queue: given where someone came in, what's the next layer of
the same room, anchored in adjacencies that are physically true in a build — the treatment goes behind the
fabric, the speakers sit within the front-of-room structure.

**A single-brand dealer is not a failed dealer.** Some accounts will only ever be one brand, and that's a
good outcome. If the trail treats them as incomplete it will push adjacency at people who don't want it —
the oversaturation risk.

### What engages a dealer: the problem, not the product

Hard-won, and it governs every piece of content. **A product on its own does nothing.** What engages a
dealer is: what problem does this solve, how does it save me time, how does it make me more money, how does
it make the experience better.

The important observation is how little dealers understand **why** to specify something. That gap isn't a
weakness to exploit — it's where engagement comes from, and explaining why to spec something is the most
valuable thing we can do in public. The first job of any piece is simply to **pique interest**, not to inform
exhaustively and certainly not to announce a product.

This is already the pattern the brands share, which is why it works as a group rule rather than an
imposition: C-ATS states three problems and gives three solutions; Fabric Walls sells removed risk and time;
DT sells technology that integrates rather than dominates; Pro-Fi describes the friction then what it did
about it. Spec sheets serve the dealer who has already decided; they don't create the decision.

## Cross-selling: the weakest link

Everything above points at the same gap, and it's worth stating bluntly: **we are currently poor at
cross-selling**, while the entire group thesis rests on it. Two legs, in order:

1. **Make buying the whole room genuinely easier** — the drawings, the known integration, parts that fit, one
   order and one delivery. Today a claim rather than an experience, because tools and data are fragmented.
   Largely Engine and canonical-dataset work; operational, not messaging.
2. **Then run the campaigns** — permissioned, adjacency-aware, timed to what the dealer is actually doing,
   with the tools supplying the *trigger* by revealing a live project and its needs.

What will **not** fix it: steering the design tools toward our own products. That breaks the tools without
closing the gap (`07-tools.md`). Build the adjacency map, close the practical gap, then market the ease —
build it, then say it.

## Where the group may surface

Because the group story is held back, it needs earned moments rather than assertion: the **shared account and
single delivery**, experienced rather than claimed; **a physical adjacency inside a real project**, the most
persuasive cross-sell there is; **the Experience Centre**, which is the whole-room story without a word of it;
and **the Screen Wall**, the one case where it genuinely is a single product rather than four brands sold
together.

`srnd.store` is the honest seam — one account across otherwise independent brands. It has to make the shared
account feel like a benefit rather than make the brands feel like departments. A design problem to work, not
a position to state.

## Touchpoints

Few enough to enumerate. Outbound: **social, email, trade press, trade events.** Plus: the **store** itself
(a touchpoint at every landing); **inbound search and AI answers**, which in a direct-global model is the top
of the funnel; **owned video and podcast**; **live virtual demo, webinar and online CE training** — Zoom doing
the rep's job and the credibility job at once; **sample kit / product in hand**, which sells a hidden product
better than any image; **peer referral**, engineered off reference jobs; and **the group's own existing
dealers**, a group-native touchpoint no single brand owns. Named channels are in `05-channels.md`.

## Execution standards

Good execution falls straight out of the journey and is mostly process — a checklist worked through without
gaps and timed right. The skill is completeness and timing, not brilliance, which is why a small team can hold
to it.

- **No gaps.** Every stage has an asset and a touchpoint; nowhere the dealer stalls.
- **Every stage hands to the next.** The core of it — no dead ends.
- **Right thing, right moment**, triggered by the dealer's state changing, not by our sending schedule.
- **Friction removed at the point of purchase.**
- **A real process for the next order**, not hoping they come back.
- **Clean cross-brand handoff** — the group must feel like one group, or the compounding leaks.

Applies at two scales: each *piece* must hook, carry, prove and hand off; and the whole *journey* must cover
every stage, wire every handoff, time every trigger.

## Metrics

The unit is **the dealer relationship, across the group, over time** — not campaign reach, not any single
brand's revenue per order. A per-brand dashboard cannot see cross-brand depth, which is the whole point.

- **On-ramp:** registrations with permission — not first orders.
- **Discovery:** Share of Model — how often a brand is named in AI answers to buyer questions.
- **Progress (not yet instrumented):** dealers reaching each stage, first orders, and **reorders and
  cross-brand orders** — the stage-6 signal that matters most. Targets set now would be guesses.
